\section{Background}

A primary application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods is the automation of tasks. When a novel AI method is developed, it is evaluated across different tasks to determine which can be automated. This practice also applies to the field of Ecology, where AI methods are increasingly employed to support data collection (\citep{besson2022towards}), data analysis, and model development (\citep{olden2008machine}), among other applications. As the popularity and accessibility of AI continue to grow, the volume of scientific research (primary literature) examining its use in ecology has also expanded. 

To synthesize and interpret this expanding body of primary literature, researchers have produced reviews (secondary literature) that summarize findings and describe the current state of AI applications. Among the various approaches to creating secondary literature, systematic reviews (including meta-analyses) and systematic maps offer the most unbiased overview of the field when conducted rigorously. However, the rapid growth of secondary literature  presents a challenge for researchers seeking to stay up to date on the topic. Also, given the field's breadth, a researcher interested in an overall summary of the subject would need to review several reviews. 

These issues can be addressed by a systematic map of reviews, which provides an overarching synthesis of the evidence collected by systematic reviews and maps (\cite{biondi2016umbrella}). As with systematic reviews, this approach includes a critical appraisal of the included reviews to evaluate their reporting or methodological rigor and identify potential sources of bias. Such appraisal is essential to avoid providing a distorted, misinformed view of the field.

The present systematic map of reviews aims to synthesise existing evidence on the applications of AI in Ecology, offering an overarching perspective on current trends, methodological approaches, and research gaps.

\section{Objectives}
In this study, we collate and assess systematic reviews and systematic maps on the use of AI methods in the field of Ecology. Our approach combines systematic mapping with appraisal and bibliometric analysis. 


We have three main objectives:
\ben
\item Reveal trends in the coverage of the systematic reviews and maps focused on the AI methods used in the broadly-defined field of Ecology, and identify existing knowledge gaps and potential biases. 
\item Perform a critical appraisal of the included systematic reviews and maps to assess their reporting quality and identify their strengths and shortcomings. 
\item Quantify the reach and impact of the systematic reviews and maps in the field through bibliometric analyzes, including locations of origin (e.g., affiliations, countries), collaboration patterns, and citations.
\een

\section{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}
\label{sec:criteria}
\subsection{Population}
Inclusion: reviews must focus on AI (or Machine Learning and Deep Learning) applications in any biological system, including organismal, or ecological levels. Studies on mixed domains are included if biological applications are a substantial focus.

Exclusion: Exclude reviews where the analytical focus is exclusively on non-biological systems (e.g., finance, engineering, computer vision without a biological application) or on humans for purely clinical/biomedical purposes, or application in biological systems on the molecular or cellular levels.

\subsection{Exposure}
Inclusion: Reviews must explicitly analyze Machine Learning (ML), Deep Learning (DL), or Artificial Intelligence (AI) as a distinct set of tools. This includes supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning methods.

Exclusion: Exclude reviews focusing solely on traditional statistical methods (e.g., linear regression, ANOVA) without framing them in the context of AI. Exclude reviews where AI is not a central theme or cannot be distinguished from other computational methods. Exclude reviews focusing only on data collection for remote sensing.

\subsection{Comparator}
Not applicable

\subsection{Outcome}
Inclusion: The review must discuss the outcomes of applying AI, such as prediction, classification, clustering, or generation of biological data. This includes outcomes such as modeling ecological systems or classifying species from images.

Exclusion: Exclude studies focused only on the theoretical or mathematical aspects of AI algorithms without discussing their application or impact in a biological context. Exclude papers on software development that do not review the outcomes of its use.

\subsection{Study type}
Inclusion: Only systematic reviews and systematic maps that synthesize primary literature, will be included (meta-analyses will also be included, although such studies are not expected). Studies must be peer-reviewed.

Exclusion: Exclude primary research articles, editorials, opinion pieces, book chapters, and narrative/scoping reviews.

\section{Screening}

We will conduct article selection for all pooled bibliographic records from the two databases searched (Web of Science and Scopus), after removing any duplicate records. We will use SysRev (https://www.sysrev.com/) for carrying out the screening in two phases. First, we will screen records based on Title, Abstract, and Keywords following a decision tree (Figure \ref{fig:titleTree}) representing our inclusion and exclusion criteria defined in Section \ref{sec:criteria}. Following this, we will collect the full texts of the provisionally included articles and perform a second round of screening based on the full texts, following a decision tree (Figure \ref{fig:fullTree}). This second round of screening will result in the list of included papers for data extraction.

To minimize errors and biases in the screening process, we will implement an independent dual screening process for both screening phases. We will resolve discrepancies through discussion or, if necessary, adjudicated by a third independent reviewer. We will document the main points of disagreement and record resaons for exclusions at the full-text screening phase.

\subsection{Pilot of literature screening}

We conducted a two-person pilot of the screening and data extraction procedures to evaluate the inclusion and exclusion criteria and the availability of the data to be extracted. We selected a random sample of $100$ articles from the Web of Science search results. We first screened this sample based on the titles and abstracts of the papers, resulting in 6 provisionally included articles. For the provisionally included papers from the first screening, we did a second screening round based on the full text, from which we included two. Based on these results, we expect around $60$ systematic reviews and systematic maps to be included in our map of reviews.

\section{Data extraction}

After the screening process, we will extract data from the included full-text reviews. One researcher will extract the data from all included full-text reviews, and a random sample (at least $10\%$) will be independently verified by a second researcher. In the following subsections, we present the data to be extracted for each of our objectives.

After extracting all variables, we will summarize and visualize the data in Python, producing figures that match the structure of each variable (e.g., box plots, network diagrams, and treemaps). For bibliometric analysis, we will use VOSviewer (\cite{van2010software}) to identify collaboration networks and citation patterns.

\subsection{Critical appraisal}

To accomplish our second objective, we will use a modified composite version of the RepOrting standards for Systematic Evidence Syntheses (ROSES) (\citep{haddaway2018roses}) for Systematic Map Reports (\citep{haddaway2017rosesMap}) and for Systematic Review Reports (\cite{haddaway2017rosesReview}). This is because our review map will likely include both systematic maps and systematic reviews. To create a composite version, we reviewed the above two ROSES checklists and selected the assessment items most relevant to our study. Table \ref{tab:ROSES} shows our ROSES-derived checklist (it specifies which checklist each item is derived from; we use both if the item is in the Map and Review Reports). Note, each element in our checklist is a Yes or No question (in few cases also Unclear), following the ROSES checklists.

As with the systematic mapping data extraction, we conducted a pilot on our benchmark papers to assess the feasibility of extracting the desired data points. We made small adjustments as needed. The average extraction time was $9$ minutes per review. Given an expected total of $60$ reviews, we estimate data extraction for the critical appraisal will take approximately $540$ minutes.
