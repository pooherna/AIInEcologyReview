\section{Background}

A primary application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods is the automation of complex or repetitive tasks. This application is particularly valuable in ecology, which often faces the daunting challenge of collecting and analyzing massive datasets. For example, manually monitoring wildlife behavior from thousands of hours of video footage or classifying species from vast acoustic records is often intractable. Consequently, AI methods are increasingly employed to automate these tasks, supporting data collection (\citep{besson2022towards}), data analysis, and model development (\citep{olden2008machine}). As AI's popularity and accessibility grow, the volume of scientific research (primary literature) examining its use in ecology has also expanded. 
%the problem

To synthesize and interpret this expanding primary literature, researchers rely on reviews (secondary literature). Rigorous approaches like systematic reviews \citep{gough2012introduction,haddaway2015making} and systematic maps \citep{james2016methodology} aim to provide the most unbiased overview. However, this very solution has created a new research bottleneck. The sheer volume of the secondary literature is now expanding, making it difficult for researchers to gain a comprehensive, up-to-date understanding of the entire topic. Without a "review of reviews", important findings and research gaps become fragmented across dozens of papers, potentially obscuring the critical next steps for the field.  
%the solution

A systematic map of reviews addresses this challenge directly by providing an overarching synthesis of the evidence from existing systematic reviews and maps \citep{biondi2016umbrella}. Similar to systematic reviews, these "maps of reviews"  include a critical appraisal of the source reviews. This appraisal evaluates the reporting or methodological rigor of the included reviews to identify potential sources of bias, which is essential to avoid providing a distorted or misinformed view of the field.

The present systematic map of reviews aims to synthesize the evidence on the applications of AI in Ecology, offering an overarching perspective that identifies current trends, methodological approaches, and research gaps.

%One of the main purposes of Artificial Intelligence (AI) methods has been to automate tasks. When a new AI method is developed, users test it on different tasks to determine what can now be automated. Reviews on the application of AI in Ecology offer a summary of how these methods are used across the field and, as such, are often used by researchers to understand the overall state of AI use in Ecology. However, with the increase in the volume of scientific research (primary literature), there has also been an increase in the number of reviews (secondary literature). Such an increase presents a challenge in staying up to date on the topic. Also, given the field's breadth, a researcher interested in an overall summary of the subject would need to review several reviews. These issues can be solved by a review of reviews, which provides an overarching view of the evidence collected by reviews \cite{biondi2016umbrella}. As with systematic reviews, a review of reviews includes a critical appraisal of the included reviews. The critical appraisal is important to identify any variability in the methodologies used in the included reviews, because disregarding these differences risks providing a distorted, misinformed view of the field.

\section{Objectives}
To achieve our aim,  our approach combines a systematic map of the literature with critical appraisal and a bibliometric analysis. This study has three main objectives:

\ben
\item Map the coverage of the included systematic reviews and maps to identify trends, knowledge gaps, and potential biases in the application of AI methods within the broadly-defined field of Ecology.
\item Perform a critical appraisal of the included systematic reviews and maps to assess their reporting quality and identify methodological strengths and shortcomings. 
\item Quantify the reach and impact of this body of literature through bibliometric analyses, detailing locations of origin (e.g., affiliations, countries), collaboration patterns, and citation networks.
\een

\section{Inclusion and exclusion criteria}
We defined the inclusion and exclusion criteria using the Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome, Study type (PECOS) framework.
\label{sec:criteria}
\subsection{Population}
Inclusion: Systematic reviews must focus on AI (or Machine Learning and Deep Learning) applications within evolutionary ecology and biological systems, including organismal and ecological levels. AI, ML and DL applications are those that are able to learn from and make decisions or predictions based on data. Reviews covering other domains (e.g., land coverage, pollution, climate change) will be included if their focus on biological applications is substantial.

Exclusion: Systematic reviews will be excluded if their analytical focus is exclusively on non-biological systems (e.g., finance, engineering, computer vision without a biological application) or on humans for purely clinical/biomedical purposes, or on biological systems but at the molecular or cellular levels.

\subsection{Exposure}
Inclusion: Systematic reviews must explicitly synthesize or evaluate Machine Learning (ML), Deep Learning (DL), or Artificial Intelligence (AI) as a distinct set of tools and how they are used in the field of biology. This includes supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning methods.

Exclusion: Systematic reviews will be excluded if they focus solely on traditional statistical methods (e.g., linear regression, ANOVA) that are not framed within the context of AI; those that do not have AI as a central theme, or if AI methods cannot be distinguished from other computational methods; reviews focus solely on remote sensing as data collection technique, without a central focus on the AI methods used to process or analyze that data.

\subsection{Comparator}
Not applicable

\subsection{Outcome}
Inclusion: The systematic review must discuss the outcomes of applying AI, including prediction, classification, clustering, or the generation of biological data. This includes outcomes such as modeling ecological systems or classifying species from images.

Exclusion: Systematic reviews that focus solely on the theoretical or mathematical aspects of AI algorithms, without discussing their application or impact in a biological context; or are primarily software development papers that do not review the outcomes of the software's application.

\subsection{Study type}
Inclusion: Only peer-reviewed systematic reviews and systematic maps that synthesize primary literature will be included. Meta-analyses will be included, although such studies are not expected. Included studies must be in English or Spanish.

Exclusion: Primary research articles, editorials, opinion pieces, book chapters, and narrative/scoping reviews will be excluded. Critically, this exclusion also applies to articles that self-described as meta-analyses or systematic reviews but do not present t a structured, reproducible synthesis of empirical (including observational) findings.

\section{Screening}

All bibliographic records from Web of Science and Scopus will be pooled and deduplicated. The subsequent screening process will be conducted in two phases using SysRev (https://www.sysrev.com/). First, all unique records will be screened based on their Title, Abstract, and Keywords. This initial screening will follow a decision tree (Figure \ref{fig:titleTree}) derived from our inclusion and exclusion criteria (Section \ref{sec:criteria}). We will retrieve the full texts for all articles provisionally included from Phase 1. These full texts will then undergo a second round of screening, using a dedicated decision tree (Figure \ref{fig:fullTree}). This final screening will determine the definitive list of included papers for data extraction.

We will implement an independent dual-screening process, at least two reviewers will independently assess each article, for both screening phases to minimize errors and biases in the screening process. The dual screening process We will resolve discrepancies through discussion or, if necessary, be adjudicated by a third independent reviewer. We will document the main points of disagreement and record reasons for exclusions at the full-text screening phase.

\subsection{Pilot of literature screening}

We conducted a two-person pilot of the screening (Sergio Poo Hernandez and Eduardo S.A. Santos)  procedure to evaluate the inclusion and exclusion criteria. We selected a random sample of $100$ articles from the Web of Science search results. We first screened this sample based on the titles and abstracts of the papers, resulting in six ($6$) provisionally included articles. For the provisionally included papers from the first screening, we conducted a second screening round based on the full text, from which we included two ($2$). Based on these results, we expect around $60$ systematic reviews and systematic maps to be included in our map of reviews.

\subsection{Critical appraisal}

To accomplish our second objective, we will use a modified composite version of the RepOrting standards for Systematic Evidence Syntheses (ROSES) (\citep{haddaway2018roses}) for Systematic Map Reports (\citep{haddaway2017rosesMap}) and for Systematic Review Reports (\cite{haddaway2017rosesReview}). This is because our review map will likely include both systematic maps and systematic reviews. To create a composite version, we reviewed the above two ROSES checklists and selected the assessment items most relevant to our study. Table \ref{tab:ROSES} shows our ROSES-derived checklist. Note, each element in our checklist is a Yes or No question (in a few cases also Partially and NA), following the ROSES checklists. The data will be extracted using a Google Form which will result in a spreadsheet containing all data.

As with the systematic mapping data extraction, we conducted a pilot on $6$ of our benchmark papers to assess the feasibility of extracting the desired data points. The pilot was conducted by a single person. The extracted data was recorded in a spreadsheet in which each column was the Topic of each data type in Table \ref{tab:ROSES}, and each row represents each document. We made small adjustments as needed. The average extraction time was $9$ minutes per review. Given an expected total of $60$ reviews, we estimate data extraction for the critical appraisal will take approximately $540$ minutes ($9$ hours).
